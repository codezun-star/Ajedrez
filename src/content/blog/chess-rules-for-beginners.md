# Chess Rules for Beginners: How Every Piece Moves

Learning how to play chess takes about fifteen minutes. Playing it well takes
years — but it starts here, with the complete rules, including the three special
moves that half-explained guides always skip and that end up causing arguments
mid-game.

## The board and the starting position

Chess is played on an 8×8 board: **64 squares** alternating light and dark. Files
are lettered a–h, ranks are numbered 1–8, so every square has a unique name — e4,
g7, a1.

Two setup rules that beginners get wrong constantly:

1. **Light square on the right.** The near-right corner must be light. If it's
   dark, the board is turned the wrong way.
2. **The queen goes on her own color.** The white queen starts on a light square
   (d1), the black queen on a dark one (d8). The king takes the square that's
   left.

Back rank, left to right: rook, knight, bishop, queen, king, bishop, knight,
rook. In front of them, eight pawns.

**White always moves first**, and then players alternate. You can never skip a
turn: if it's your move, you must move.

## How each piece moves

| Piece | How it moves | Approximate value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pawn | One square forward (two on its first move) | 1 |
| Knight | An L-shape: two squares, then one at a right angle | 3 |
| Bishop | Diagonally, any distance | 3 |
| Rook | Horizontally or vertically, any distance | 5 |
| Queen | Rook and bishop combined | 9 |
| King | One square in any direction | — |

### The pawn

The strangest piece on the board, because it **captures differently from how it
moves**: it advances straight ahead but captures one square diagonally. It never
moves backwards.

From its starting square it may advance one or two squares. If a piece sits
directly in front of it, the pawn is stuck — it can neither capture it nor jump
over it.

### The knight

Moves in an L — two squares one way, one square perpendicular — and is **the only
piece that jumps**. Whatever stands in between is irrelevant; only the
destination matters. A knight in the centre reaches up to eight squares; in a
corner, just two. Hence the old rule: *a knight on the rim is dim*.

### The bishop

Travels full diagonals. Each bishop stays forever on the colour it started on, so
the two complement each other — which is why **the bishop pair** counts as a
small advantage.

### The rook

Moves along ranks and files. It's worth a lot but takes time to matter: rooks
come alive when files open and the endgame approaches.

### The queen

Rook and bishop in one, and the strongest piece on the board. Which is exactly
why you **shouldn't bring her out early**: your opponent attacks her with minor
pieces and develops for free while you run.

### The king

Moves one square in any direction. It is never captured — the goal is to trap it.
And one rule is absolute: **you may never make a move that leaves your own king
in check**.

## The three special moves

### Castling

Castling moves two pieces at once, king and rook, and it's the best way to get
your king to safety. The king travels **two squares** toward the rook, and the
rook hops over and lands beside it.

It's only legal if **all** of this holds:

- Neither the king nor **that** rook has moved at any point in the game.
- There are no pieces between them.
- The king **is not currently in check**.
- The king does **not pass through** or **land on** an attacked square.

One detail that gets confused constantly: **the rook may be attacked**, and it
may cross an attacked square. The restriction applies to the king only.

There are two versions: **kingside castling** (short, three squares between) and
**queenside castling** (long — slower, sometimes sharper).

### En passant

When an enemy pawn advances **two squares at once** and lands directly beside one
of your pawns, you may capture it as though it had only moved one: your pawn
takes it and finishes on the square it skipped.

The catch: **only on your very next move**. Play anything else and the right is
gone forever. It's the only capture in chess where the captured piece isn't on
the destination square.

### Promotion

A pawn reaching the far rank **must transform** into a queen, rook, bishop or
knight of the same colour. It cannot stay a pawn and cannot become a king.

Almost everyone picks a queen. The classic exception is promoting to a knight
when that delivers checkmate or forks your way out of trouble. And yes, you can
have **two or more queens** at once.

## How a game ends

### Check and checkmate

**Check** means your king is under attack. You must respond, and there are only
three legal answers: move the king, capture the attacking piece, or block the
line between them (blocking never works against a knight).

If your king is in check and **none** of the three is available, that's
**checkmate** and the game is over.

### Draws

Nearly a third of games between evenly matched players end in a draw. There are
five ways:

- **Stalemate**: it's your move, you are **not** in check, and you have no legal
  move at all. That's a draw, not a loss — a lifeline when you're losing.
- **Insufficient material**: not enough pieces left to force mate (king vs king,
  king and bishop, king and knight…).
- **Threefold repetition**: the same position occurs three times with the same
  player to move.
- **Fifty-move rule**: fifty moves by each side with no pawn move and no capture.
- **Agreement** between the two players.

## What each piece is worth

The values in the table above exist to help you decide trades: swapping a bishop
(3) for a rook (5) nets you **+2**, which is called winning the exchange.

They're a guide, not a law. A knight planted in the centre can outperform a
trapped rook, and in endgames with pawns on both wings bishops usually beat
knights. But while you're learning, counting points prevents most disasters.

## Put it into practice

Rules stick by playing, not by rereading. Start a game against the AI on Easy and
watch three things: castle early, leave nothing undefended, and check every check
before you move.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who moves first in chess?
White, always. After that players alternate, and skipping a turn isn't allowed — if it's your move, you have to make one.

### Can you capture the king?
No. The king is never captured. When it's attacked that's check, and you must resolve it; if there's no legal way to do so, it's checkmate and the game ends.

### Can a pawn promote to any piece?
To a queen, rook, bishop or knight of the same colour. It cannot remain a pawn and cannot become a king, and promotion is compulsory on reaching the final rank.

### What happens if a player has no legal moves?
If they're also in check, it's checkmate and they lose. If they're not in check, it's stalemate and the game is a draw.

### Can I castle if my rook is attacked?
Yes. The attacked-square restriction applies only to the king: it cannot be in check, pass through an attacked square, or land on one. The rook may be attacked and may cross attacked squares freely.
