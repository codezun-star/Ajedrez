# Chess Tactics: 8 Patterns That Win Games

Here's an uncomfortable fact: **below 1800 Elo, almost every game is decided by
tactics, not strategy**. Someone leaves a piece undefended, the other player
either sees it or doesn't, and that's the game. The good news is that tactics
aren't talent — they're eight patterns you learn to recognise.

## What a tactic actually is

A tactic is a **short, forcing sequence** that wins material or delivers mate. The
key word is *forcing*: your opponent has no reasonable alternatives. It almost
always rests on one of two weaknesses:

- A piece left **undefended** (hanging).
- Two valuable pieces **lined up** or sitting close together.

Train your eye for those two things and the eight patterns start appearing on
their own.

## 1. Fork (double attack with one piece)

A single piece attacks two targets at once. The **knight** is the undisputed king
of forks because its L-shaped move jumps over defenders and hits squares no other
piece covers the same way.

The most profitable fork is the one hitting **king and queen**: your opponent must
save the king and loses the queen with no way out.

Pawns fork too (push a pawn that attacks two pieces at once), as do queens and
rooks.

## 2. Pin

A piece can't move because **something more valuable sits behind it**. Pins are
made by line pieces: bishop, rook and queen.

There are two kinds and the difference matters:

- **Absolute pin:** the **king** is behind. The pinned piece legally cannot move.
  It's frozen.
- **Relative pin:** a queen or rook is behind. Moving is legal, but it costs
  material.

How to cash in: **attack the pinned piece again**. It can't run, so adding one
more attacker wins material.

## 3. Skewer (the pin in reverse)

The valuable piece is **in front** and, when it moves to safety, it drops the one
behind it. The classic case: you check a king with a rook directly behind it; the
king is forced to move and you collect the rook.

## 4. Discovered attack

You move one piece and **the piece behind it** reveals an attack. What makes it
brutal is that you create two threats with one move: the moving piece can attack
something itself while the piece behind attacks something else.

## 5. Double check

The most violent version of the discovery: **two pieces give check at once**.
Capturing and blocking are both useless, because one move can't answer two checks.
**The king must move, always.** That's why double check shows up in so many mating
combinations.

## 6. Deflection

You force a piece to **abandon the square or the job it was doing**. If a knight
is the only thing defending your mating square, attack it with something it can't
ignore: once it moves, the defence collapses.

## 7. Decoy

The opposite of deflection: you **drag** a piece (usually the king) onto a bad
square, almost always with a sacrifice. A queen check the king is forced to
capture can pull it onto exactly the square where a knight fork is waiting.

## 8. Overloading

One piece has **two jobs at once** and can't manage both. If a rook is defending
your back rank *and* a knight, attack the knight: the rook has to choose.

## How to spot them in your own games

The trick isn't calculating more, it's **looking better**. Three questions before
every move:

1. **Which pieces are undefended?** Theirs and yours. Nearly every tactic starts
   here.
2. **Are two valuable pieces lined up?** On the same rank, file or diagonal.
   That's where pins and skewers live.
3. **What is each of their pieces defending?** If something defends two things,
   it's overloaded.

And one golden rule for calculating: **check the forcing moves first** — checks,
captures and threats, in that order. Most tactics begin with a check or a capture
that looked absurd.

## A realistic training plan

- **15 minutes a day** of tactics puzzles beats three hours at the weekend.
- When you get one wrong, **don't move on**: understand why the right move worked.
- In your own games, export the **PGN** and hunt for the tactics you missed —
  yours and your opponent's.

Start a game against the AI with a single goal: find one knight fork. Once you've
seen it happen, you'll see it forever.

## Frequently asked questions

### What's the most common tactic in chess?
The knight fork, especially the one hitting king and queen together. It's the most frequent below 1500 Elo because the knight's jump is the hardest move to visualise.

### What's the difference between a pin and a skewer?
In a pin the valuable piece is behind, so the piece in front can't move. In a skewer the valuable piece is in front: when it moves to safety, it drops the piece behind it.

### Why is double check so strong?
Because you can't answer it by capturing the attacker or blocking the line — there are two simultaneous checks and one move can't solve both. The king is forced to move, no matter what.

### How much tactics training should I do daily?
Fifteen minutes a day is the sweet spot for most players. Consistency matters far more than volume: short daily sessions beat one long session a week.

### How do I see tactics faster in my games?
Before each move, look for undefended pieces and for valuable pieces lined up, and calculate the forcing moves first: checks, captures and threats, in that order. Almost every tactic starts with one of the three.
